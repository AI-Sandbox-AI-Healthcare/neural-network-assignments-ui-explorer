🏥 The Problem

Identify patients with chronic pain from their electronic health records (EHR). Chronic pain affects roughly 20% of adults but is often under-detected until crisis — early identification enables proactive care and cuts long-term costs.

It's still a binary classification task (pain = 1 or pain = 0). What's new: address the class-imbalance issue, and the model is more than one linear line — a Random Forest first, then a small feed-forward network you design.

📊 The Dataset: Same 320 Patients

The exact Assignment 1 dataset, label, and personal seed. 19 usable numeric features per patient. Your NetID hashes (SHA-256 → 100–999) to the same train/validation split the pipeline and autograder use.

🔬 The Task: Building on Assignment 1

Assignment 1 built one logistic-regression line from scratch. Assignment 2 adds three things: measure the class imbalance and correct it (class weights or oversampling); train a Random Forest and read which clinical signals it relies on; then design a small feed-forward network, testing depth vs. width, ReLU vs. tanh, dropout, and early stopping one change at a time.

Oversampling duplicates minority rows with replacement. At ratio 1.0 both classes have the same row count — that is what random_oversample() produces.

Target minority : majority ratio — what it does

random_oversample() copies chronic-pain (minority) rows until they reach this fraction of the majority-class count. Only the training set changes — the validation set keeps the real ~40/60 mix so scores stay honest. Slide the control on the left; here is what three settings look like:

ratio 0.00
→ raw data. The model barely sees pain cases and defaults to “no pain”: high accuracy, poor recall & F1.
ratio 0.50
→ half-balanced. Minority padded to 50% of the majority. Recall climbs; a few more false alarms appear.
ratio 1.00
→ fully balanced. Equal counts — the best F1 for most seeds, and exactly what random_oversample() produces.
